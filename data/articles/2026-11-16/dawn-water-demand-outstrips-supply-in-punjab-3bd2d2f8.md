# Water demand outstrips supply in Punjab: UN agency

• FAO report says province losing 5.5bn cubic metres of groundwater annually 
• Highlights growing need for water resource management 
• Recommends setting up commission to review policies, existing strategies
• Identifies shortcomings in data collection, info consolidation
ISLAMABAD: A detailed assessment of Punjab’s water resources by a United Nations specialised agency has warned of serious challenges ahead, saying the province’s water demand already exceeds supply across all sectors.
The Food and Agriculture Organisation (FAO) said groundwater was being depleted by about 5.5 billion cubic metres annually over a five-year period, while surface water resources were also under growing pressure.
To assess the province’s water stress, the FAO sought expert advice from water specialists, including former Water and Power Development Authority (Wapda) chairman Shamsul Mulk and former Indus River System Authority (Irsa) chairman Rao Irshad Ali Khan.
The findings, published in a report titled Water and Land Accounting in Punjab, were based on annual water accounts covering five water years from 2012 to 2017. The report highlighted the growing need for comprehensive water resources management, saying Punjab required more advanced and integrated water services across different sectors and regions.
It strongly recommended the establishment of a commission to conduct a one-time review of provincial water resources, water-related policies, institutional mandates and existing strategies.
The commission’s tasks would include ensuring coherence between national and inter-provincial policies and water initiatives, and establishing an integrated mechanism to assess the impact of transboundary water projects and other interventions.
It would also identify overlapping responsibilities and changes needed in institutional mandates, assess water and land resources, current demand and projections until 2050, and establish a provincial coordination cell within the Planning and Development Department for day-to-day operations.
Given Punjab’s major contribution to the national economy, the management of its water resources is particularly critical, especially as Pakistan’s overall water supply is considered insecure.
The gap between water supply and demand has become particularly acute in Punjab’s urban areas because of limited access to freshwater and declining groundwater reserves, the report observed.
Agricultural production is also increasingly at risk because of water stress and the depletion of groundwater storage, it said. Freshwater shortages have led to greater reliance on the recycling of low-quality water, while pollution from agro-chemicals and urban wastewater has intensified.
The analysis estimated that Punjab needed an additional 10 billion cubic metres of usable water annually to bridge the supply-demand gap and sustain its groundwater aquifer.
It stressed the need for timely intervention, including exploring ways to increase surface water resources, improve groundwater recharge and develop provincial mechanisms for inter-seasonal water transfers.
The report said rivers flowing through Punjab and its freshwater bodies had diminished significantly, with river flows declining by about 30 billion cubic metres following the 1960 Indus Waters Treaty with India.
The province has only 1.7 per cent of wetland areas, while its natural landscape has declined considerably. Rainfall remains the only source of water outside canal command areas and urban centres, it noted.
The report also observed that groundwater recharge sources had diminished, reducing the province’s net renewable water resources.
Punjab’s canal system operates as a run-of-the-river system for half the year and cannot draw its full authorised share of river water. “The reduction in drainage flows suggests a consistent water supply and demand gap,” it added.
A technical diagnosis of the water accounts identified four main challenges: increasing spatio-temporal water scarcity; continuous groundwater depletion; competition among sectors for water and unaccounted inter-sectoral water transfers; and the need to protect annual renewable water resources, wetlands and the natural landscape.
The report also identified shortcomings in data collection and information consolidation. It recommended expanding the land-use classification system to cover the entire province.
