# Islamabad to host Makkah alliance summit later this month: DPM Dar

Published November 16, 2026
Deputy Prime Minister and Foreign Minister Ishaq Dar said on Monday that the top-level security committee of the Makkah defence agreement was expected to meet in Islamabad later this month.
Addressing a press briefing, following the Economic Cooperation Organisation (ECO) ministerial meeting in Islamabad, Dar said: "The apex committee of this body... will be actually meeting, hopefully, end of this month in Islamabad."
Dar said Pakistan was scheduled to host the next meeting alongside Saudi Arabia and Turkiye, the other two signatories to the agreement, which was signed in Makkah on August 7. The three nations pledged to provide mutual assistance if any one of them faces an armed attack or military aggression.
Saudi Arabia has faced repeated attacks by the Houthi rebels in Yemen, with the group targeting cities, oil facilities and shipping since fighting in Yemen's civil war intensified in July.
The attacks coincided with the expansion of the war in the Middle East, which began with US-Israeli strikes on Iran in February and has since drawn in countries across the region.
The DPM said the trilateral defence agreement between Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Turkiye was based on the principle of self-defence and did not violate the United Nations Charter, citing Article 51, which recognises the right of individual and collective self-defence.
He said the agreement's fundamental principle was that an attack on one member would be considered an attack on all three. Its objective was to strengthen defence cooperation and collective security among the signatories, adding that it should not be interpreted as an indication that the three countries sought confrontation with other nations.
Dar said the region should develop its own security system and architecture to address regional security challenges, adding that a collective security mechanism was essential for maintaining peace and stability.
He said work was underway to establish an institutional framework for the agreement, with its inaugural meeting held in Istanbul on August 31. A working committee would also be established to oversee its implementation, comprising one representative from the Foreign Office, three secretaries and one military representative.
The agreement provides that an armed attack against any one of the three members would be treated as an attack against all, committing the signatories to extend mutual assistance in response to external aggression.
Its first article requires the parties to employ "all necessary means" to address threats to one another's territorial integrity, sovereignty and internationally recognised borders, according to the agreement's text seen by Reuters.
Article 2 states that if any signatory faces an armed attack or military aggression by another country or group of countries, the other parties must immediately provide assistance through the available measures of mutual support.
The provision, which resembles Nato's Article 5 collective defence clause, invokes Article 51 of the United Nations Charter, which recognises the right of individual and collective self-defence against armed attacks.
The agreement says the nature and extent of assistance will be determined after an objective assessment of requirements, with each party contributing within its available resources, including military capabilities.
The agreement bars any of the three signatories from withdrawing during its first 10 years of entry into force. After that period, a party seeking to leave must provide at least three months' written notice to the others through diplomatic channels.
The pact is set to remain in force indefinitely, subject to its withdrawal provisions. A member's departure would not affect projects or activities agreed upon before its withdrawal, while confidentiality obligations concerning classified information would continue to apply.
The agreement also requires signatories to protect classified documents and information obtained under its provisions, prohibiting their disclosure or transfer to states, companies or individuals outside the parties.
Any amendments would require consultations and unanimous approval from the agreement's designated core parties.
The agreement envisages cooperation in the defence industry, intelligence sharing, logistical support and counterterrorism, including at the operational level. It also provides for joint military training and collaboration in research, development and advanced technologies.
At an emergency meeting of the alliance in Riyadh earlier this month, the three countries agreed to ensure the rapid deployment of military forces and capabilities in Saudi Arabia amid Houthi attacks.
Turkiye has condemned the attacks on Saudi Arabia, offered support for Riyadh's military technology requirements and agreed to join a Saudi-led coalition aimed at safeguarding navigation in the Red Sea.
Turkiye's formal approval of the agreement is subject to parliamentary ratification.
Diplomatic efforts to end the war involving Iran have yet to produce a breakthrough. The conflict began with surprise US-Israeli strikes on Tehran that killed Iran's Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei.
Over more than eight months of fighting, Iran has restricted maritime traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, a vital route for global oil shipments, while targeting infrastructure and US assets across the Middle East.
Bahrain came under a second Iranian drone attack in three days on Sunday, ending a prolonged lull in strikes on the country during the conflict.
On Friday, Iran's Revolutionary Guards said they had targeted a US early-warning radar system in Bahrain. The announcement followed US strikes on Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz earlier this month.
