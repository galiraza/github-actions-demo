# 'They have decided to fight me for speaking truth': Bilawal counters Centre's criticism

Published November 18, 2026
The ongoing debate over the creation of new provinces took a fresh turn on Wednesday night as Pakistan Peoples Party (PPP) Chairman Bilawal Bhutto-Zardari took a swipe at the Centre, saying that he will face opposition from the politicians as they lacked courage to hear the truth.
"It appears they [federal ministers] have decided to pick a fight with me for speaking the truth. The reaction [to his remarks] shows they are unwilling to tolerate criticism," Bilawal said while responding to a series of statements by federal ministers earlier in the day.
The PPP chief questioned how ordinary citizens could expect their voices to reach the government if politicians were unwilling to listen to him.
The federal ministers had strongly criticised Bilawal's opposition to the proposed division of Sindh, arguing that the creation of smaller provinces was necessary for better governance and that public opinion should prevail in the matter.
Defence Minister Khawaja Asif stressed that no political system could be changed without public support, while Federal Minister for Health Mustafa Kamal described smaller provinces as inevitable, saying that the country could not function effectively without them.
Other ministers, including Talal Chaudhry, also questioned Bilawal's stance, pointing to the PPP's position on the division of Punjab and arguing that democracy required effective governance, not merely political speeches and constitutional arguments.
Speaking about the debate, Bilawal said he was offering suggestions as a major stakeholder to improve the situation and urged political leaders to exercise caution. He said the discussion on new provinces had begun without consulting any minister, the prime minister or other political stakeholders.
“If you have initiated this debate, have the courage to hold it openly. It cannot be conducted through your own selected people, with everyone else expected to agree or remain silent,” he said.
The PPP chairman said the discussion should also focus on the country's fundamental problems rather than being confined to the creation of new provinces. He questioned who had the authority to declare that the system had collapsed and decide that it needed to be reset.
“Does the PCB chairman have the authority to say that the system has collapsed? Has the responsibility for deciding on a reset been handed to the PCB?” he asked.
Bilawal also referred to the political situation in Sindh during the 1980s and 1990s, saying attempts had been made to create divisions in the province at the time. He warned that the risk remained and said his earlier remarks were intended as a warning, not a threat.
“I was sounding the alarm, not issuing a threat. I was warning about the consequences if such a move was forced through,” he said, adding that coercion could trigger unrest that would be difficult to contain.
Turning to constitutional reforms, Bilawal described the establishment of a constitutional court under the 27th Amendment as a historic achievement, saying he would not backtrack on the amendment. He clarified that he was not calling for the amendment or constitutional courts to be abolished.
The PPP chairman said a political and democratic reset required restoring the opposition's trust. He proposed returning reserved seats to the opposition, stressing that it was a suggestion and not a red line.
“If we want to make a start, this is where we can begin,” he said, adding that the PPP had supported the government even when those seats were not in its hands. He said the party would not suffer a major setback if the seats were not returned.
Bilawal said he could offer suggestions but could not force the government to accept them. Questioning claims that a decision on creating new provinces had already been made, he asked why his proposals were drawing criticism.
“I would remind the government that the power to say 'Be, and it is' belongs only to Allah,” he said, warning that declaring the matter settled would have consequences.
Despite his reservations about the government's treatment of its coalition partners, Bilawal said he continued to support the government and wanted Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif to succeed. He said the public's main expectation from political leaders was a solution to economic problems.
He also questioned whether the PPP's political future depended on holding the offices of governor or Senate chairman.
Referring to PTI founder Imran Khan, Bilawal asked whether he could not be considered a political prisoner when he was both a politician and an inmate.
“I find it difficult to accept that every prime minister of this country is a thief. I want to see a day when someone becomes prime minister of Pakistan and does not end up in jail,” he added.
