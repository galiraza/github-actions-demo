# World's top jurists' organisation questions judicial independence in Pakistan after 26th, 27th amendments

Published November 18, 2026
The International Commission of Jurists (ICJ) released a report on Wednesday examining the state of Pakistan’s judiciary following the 26th and 27th amendments, concluding that a series of constitutional and legislative changes has undermined judicial independence in the country.
The jurists maintained that the legislation had impaired the judiciary’s capacity to act as an effective check on the government and to provide effective remedies for violations of human rights, according to the ICJ report.
“The 26th and 27th amendments have done exactly what we feared: they have placed Pakistan’s highest courts under the control of the executive. When the courts are captured, no one is left to hold the government to account, and victims of human rights violations have nowhere to turn,” said ICJ’s Secretary-General Santiago Canton.
The report analyses the institutional framework governing the high courts, the Supreme Court (SC), and the Federal Constitutional Court (FCC) in light of Pakistan’s obligations under international law a year after the 27th Amendment was passed in November 2025, and two years after the 26th Amendment was passed in October 2024.
The 26th Amendment introduced major changes to the country’s judiciary, taking away the Supreme Court’s suo motu powers, setting the chief justice of Pakistan’s (CJP) term at three years, and empowering the prime minister to appoint the next CJP from among the three most senior Supreme Court judges.
Passed in November 2025, the 27th Amendment made significant changes to the judicial system, including establishing the Federal Constitutional Court, changing the military’s chain of command and granting immunities to top government officials.
In the report, the ICJ found that the cumulative effect of the amendments and their implementation is that Pakistan’s highest courts no longer enjoy the institutional guarantees of independence required by Article 14 of the ICCPR and the UN Basic Principles on the Independence of the Judiciary, among other international standards.
It said the constitutional amendments impaired the right of victims of human rights violations to an effective remedy, guaranteed under Article 2(3) of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR), to which Pakistan is a party, as well as under Pakistan’s domestic law.
The ICJ has long advocated for reforms to make the judiciary in Pakistan more independent and accountable, including through an appointment process that is transparent and inclusive. It has also raised concerns about judicial overreach and encroachment into the domains of Parliament and other institutions.
“Pakistan’s judiciary needed reform. Judges were appointed without transparency, and at times the courts overstepped into the domain of other institutions. None of these problems has been fixed. Instead, the Government used people’s legitimate frustration with the courts as a pretext to bring them under its full control,” Santiago Canton added.
The structural problems identified in the report require substantial constitutional and statutory amendments, and the ICJ recommends revising or repealing provisions in the 26th and 27th amendments that are inconsistent with international law and standards on the independence of the judiciary.
