# 'We don't want to die': Protesters slam Claude creator Anthropic after IPO debut

- Source: Dawn
- Published: Fri, 20 Nov 2026 19:46:57 +0500
- Link: https://www.dawn.com/news/2045928/we-dont-want-to-die-protesters-slam-claude-creator-anthropic-after-ipo-debut
- Collected: 2026-11-20 15:53:36 UTC

---

Around a hundred people braved the rain on Thursday to protest in front of Anthropic’s offices in San Francisco, the same day the artificial intelligence (AI) lab went public, to denounce its race toward ever more powerful AI models.
“Ban RSI, we don’t want to die,” the protesters chanted.
They were referring to recursive self-improvement (RSI) — a term the industry uses to describe building advanced AI models that can autonomously create newer versions of themselves, which critics worry will hasten doomsday scenarios.
“Don’t invest in extinction,” one sign said.
“These people are going to make a profit off of a technology that even they admit has a chance of eliminating life on Earth, or human life on Earth,” Tarnel Abbott, a 72-year-old retired librarian, said at Thursday’s protest.
Another protester, Emily Tan, who travelled from Los Angeles, described the situation as “a black box.”
“Most people in the country, even in SF, do not know what is happening,” Tan, 31, said.
The protesters’ first demand: ban RSI.
“There are only a few places in the world where recursive self-improvement is possible in 2027, and this office is one of them,” Jacob Coxon said.
Coxon is a young researcher whose resignation from Anthropic, and accompanying warnings about AI safety, caused a major stir back in September.
People in Silicon Valley admit in private that they don’t want RSI to be developed anytime soon, Coxon said.
Anthropic, which on Thursday pulled off the largest IPO in history, says it has not yet reached the RSI stage.
The ‘good guys’
Critics question whether a company like Anthropic, which talks about prioritising safety over profits, can stick to its stated values while facing pressure from shareholders, who want to see growth and a return on their investment.
“They are talking about trying to slow this down and doing pacing, but (…) they’re not, like, trying that hard,” said Tyler Tracy, 26, a researcher at an AI safety nonprofit called Redwood Research.
Anthropic was founded by several former OpenAI employees who disagreed with its business decisions and vowed to always prioritise safety.
“Stop the AI race,” another protest sign said.
Daniel Kokotajlo, a former OpenAI employee, said friends at both labs answer his fears by arguing that if they don’t build it, a rival or China will, and that they are “the responsible good guys”.
“I don’t like that answer, and that’s why I’m here protesting,” Kokotajlo said. He now heads a research organisation called the AI Futures Project.
Many of the protesters acknowledged they still use AI tools and consider them helpful, but they feel it is advancing too quickly and without sufficient safeguards.
A day earlier, an open letter signed by researchers, entrepreneurs, and academics accused Anthropic of not being “a safe AI company.”
A sole counter-protester, 31-year-old activist blogger Barak Gila, stood in front of the crowd holding a sign that thanked Anthropic for building the technology and generating a sizable local economic impact.
A demonstrator confronted him, but Gila was all smiles, even delighted at the prospect of a clash.
“Without Anthropic, I’m gonna die for sure, right? With Anthropic, maybe I’ll live 10 years longer,” Gila said.
“Or maybe you’ll die in like two years,” the other demonstrator responded.
