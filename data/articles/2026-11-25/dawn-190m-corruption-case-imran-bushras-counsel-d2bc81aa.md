# £190m corruption case: Imran, Bushra's counsel says transaction caused no loss to exchequer

- Source: Dawn
- Published: Wed, 25 Nov 2026 16:55:33 +0500
- Link: https://www.dawn.com/news/2046995/ps190m-corruption-case-imran-bushras-counsel-says-transaction-caused-no-loss-to-exchequer
- Collected: 2026-11-25 17:45:43 UTC

---

ISLAMABAD: In the £190 million corruption case, counsel for PTI founder and former prime minister Imran Khan and his wife, Bushra Bibi, argued that the transaction had caused no loss to the national exchequer but had instead proved financially beneficial.
The couple is serving prison sentences in the £190 million corruption case, also known as the Al-Qadir Trust case. On January 17, 2025, an accountability court in Islamabad sentenced Imran to 14 years in prison and Bushra to seven years in the case. Both subsequently challenged their convictions before the IHC.
The development came as the Islamabad High Court (IHC) resumed hearing appeals filed by Imran and Bushra against their convictions on Wednesday.
A division bench comprising IHC Chief Justice Sardar Muhammad Sarfraz Dogar and Justice Muhammad Asif heard the appeals.
At the outset, Barrister Salman Safdar, counsel for the appellants, sought live streaming of the proceedings, arguing that the facility was available in courts across Pakistan and in other countries.
He said the request was aimed at ensuring transparency in a case involving matters of public importance. The counsel clarified that he was not seeking a telecast of the proceedings on Pakistan Television but wanted them to be live-streamed for public access.
Barrister Safdar pointed out that the trial had been conducted behind closed doors inside Adiala jail, arguing that open, live-streamed proceedings before the IHC would help address the lack of public access during the trial.
Justice Dogar observed that arguments concerning the May 9 incident and other general matters were irrelevant to the proceedings. He advised the counsel to focus on the pending application and the legal issues involved in the appeals.
Justice Dogar also pointed out that the IHC proceedings were already open to the public and that the courtroom was crowded.
When Barrister Safdar mentioned that Imran was 74 years old and incarcerated, the chief justice advised him to advance legal arguments rather than refer to general information about the appellant.
The counsel cited the case of former military ruler General (retd) Pervez Musharraf and read excerpts from Indian court judgments in support of his plea for live-streaming.
However, Justice Dogar observed that rules governing live-streaming were yet to be finalised and that the application could not be allowed at this stage.
Barrister Safdar requested the court to adjourn the application until the rules were framed, expressing hope that the matter would be resolved soon. He said that, as an advocate, he considered it appropriate to advance legal arguments even if the judge was initially reluctant to accept his request.
The chief justice observed that the conduct of proceedings was at the court’s discretion.
Arguing the main appeals, Barrister Safdar said the prosecution had produced 35 witnesses during the trial and Imran had been convicted under various provisions of the National Accountability Ordinance.
He elaborated that Imran was formally arrested in the case on November 13, 2023, while Bushra was convicted of abetment and arrested after her conviction.
The counsel pointed out that several individuals, including former accountability adviser Mirza Shahzad Akbar, property tycoon Malik Riaz, his son Ahmed Ali Riaz, Zulfikar Bukhari, Farhat Shahzadi and Ziaul Mustafa Nasim, had been declared absconders in the case.
Reading from the charge sheet, he said Akbar had produced a letter from the UK’s National Crime Agency (NCA) regarding the freezing of accounts belonging to Riaz and his family members.
He said the accounts had been frozen in three phases and maintained that the alleged financial benefits to Imran and Bushra had been extended before the accounts were frozen.
He also referred to the prosecution’s allegation that Bahria Town had extended financial benefits to Imran and Bushra through Bukhari in April 2019 under the guise of donations.
However, he pointed out that Riaz’s money had been frozen on August 12, 2019. Initially, the allegation against Imran was one of abetment, but Imran was subsequently made the principal accused, the counsel added.
According to the prosecution’s case, Imran allegedly approved the transfer of the frozen funds to an account maintained by the Supreme Court in violation of the Rules of Business. The funds were linked to Rs460 billion that Riaz was supposed to deposit in connection with the Bahria Town land dispute in Karachi.
Barrister Safdar argued that the amount had been transferred to the SC account even before the Federal Cabinet’s approval.
He alleged that Akbar and Nasim, both absconders in the case, had played a role in placing the relevant summary before the cabinet and facilitating the transfer.
The counsel further added that the prosecution’s case was that the amount should have been deposited with the State Bank of Pakistan (SBP) rather than the apex court account to settle a civil liability.
Referring to the Al-Qadir University land transaction, he said the land had been transferred in January 2021. The prosecution had alleged that Riaz provided Rs285 million for the university’s building and furniture, but the amount was never transferred to Imran or Bushra’s personal accounts, the counsel argued.
The counsel said Imran was accused of chairing a cabinet meeting on December 3, 2019, and misleading its members into approving the transfer to benefit Riaz.
He maintained that the funds were deposited in the SC account and subsequently invested in profitable ventures, generating substantial returns.
“The amount remained with the Supreme Court for four years, and everyone knew about it,” he argued, adding that the funds had yielded significant profits.
Barrister Safdar further argued that Akbar had held multiple positions, including special assistant to the prime minister, chairman of the Assets Recovery Unit and adviser on accountability. He alleged that Akbar, in connivance with others, had played a key role in signing the confidentiality agreement that paved the way for the recovery of the frozen funds.
He contended that there was no evidence establishing Imran’s active knowledge of any alleged wrongdoing and that neither Imran nor Bushra had signed the confidentiality agreement.
He also questioned how Imran could have been aware of any alleged irregularities when the National Accountability Bureau (NAB) itself was part of the Assets Recovery Unit.
When Justice Dogar observed that the Assets Recovery Unit was an independent entity, Barrister Safdar responded that it comprised officials from NAB, the Federal Investigation Agency and SBP.
“If these officials were unaware of any wrongdoing, how could Imran Khan have known about it?” he questioned.
The counsel argued that the transaction did not cause any loss to the national exchequer, as the amount had remained within the banking system and had almost doubled through investments.
He maintained that the case could not be characterised as one involving a financial loss to the national exchequer, arguing instead that the transaction had generated profits. He also argued that the costly prosecution itself had burdened taxpayers.
Questioning the prosecution’s reliance on alleged collusion, he said the trial had concluded while several key suspects remained absent and had been declared absconders, raising questions about how the alleged conspiracy and connivance had been established.
Regarding Bushra, Barrister Safdar argued that she had no direct connection with the transaction and that neither the investigation nor the trial court’s judgment had clearly explained her specific role in the alleged offence.
He further contended that there was no evidence establishing that the money frozen in the United Kingdom represented proceeds of crime. If the funds were illicit or proceeds of crime, there could be legal consequences; otherwise, their owner was entitled to deal with the money at his discretion, he argued.
The counsel described both the reference and the charge sheet as vague, maintaining that the prosecution had failed to establish the essential ingredients of the offences against the appellants.
The court adjourned further hearings of the appeals until next week.
