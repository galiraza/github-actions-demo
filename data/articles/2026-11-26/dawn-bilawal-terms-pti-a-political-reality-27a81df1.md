# Bilawal terms PTI a ‘political reality’, calls for restoring its trust in the ‘system’

- Source: Dawn
- Published: Thu, 26 Nov 2026 21:18:10 +0500
- Link: https://www.dawn.com/news/2047216/bilawal-terms-pti-a-political-reality-calls-for-restoring-its-trust-in-the-system
- Collected: 2026-11-26 17:29:52 UTC

---

PPP Chairman Bilawal Bhutto-Zardari on Thursday asserted that PTI was a “political reality” whose trust in the “system” needs to be restored for a political reset to take place.
Bilawal made the remarks while speaking at another “Meet the Press” event, this time at the Multan Press Club. The Bhutto scion is on a visit to Punjab for the past few days, making stops in Multan, Dera Ghazi Khan and Muzaffargarh so far.
Responding to a query about him calling former premier Imran Khan a “political prisoner”, Bilawal explained that he made the “controversial” statement in a polarised political environment, where those terming him a political prisoner could not be convinced otherwise and those declaring him a convict and corrupt could not be convinced otherwise.
Bilawal stressed that in his speech, not the press queries, he made an observation that was not about Imran, but rather about Pakistan’s political history wherein elected premiers often end up going to jail later on or come to power after being imprisoned.
“Whatever opinion you may hold about Khan sahib, even if you think he is not a political prisoner, there is no denying the fact that PTI is a political reality,” the PPP chairman stressed.
He emphasised that even if Imran was considered a “convicted felon or terrorist or whatever”, prisoners in a respectable society were entitled to their rights.
Bilawal said that although the PTI founder “tried to deny my family those rights”, he would not want to continue the cycle of “repeating the same mistakes” in vengeance.
“Khan sahib is a convict, he is in jail, but PTI is a political reality. Until PTI’s trust is restored in this system […] and they are able to play their role in the Parliament, the reset that is being discussed cannot take place in that manner,” he maintained.
The PPP chairman’s latest remarks come amid a growing debate over a “system reset” in the country. In July, Interior Minister Mohsin Naqvi stirred a political storm by calling for the creation of new administrative units, arguing that the current system of governance had “collapsed” and needed a “reset”.
On Nov 17, in a hard-hitting speech, Bilawal had asserted that the governance system had not collapsed, but was rather “made to collapse”.
In a similar vein, the Bhutto scion on Monday had called for restoring the supremacy of Parliament as well as trust in the judiciary and the electoral system.
During Thursday’s address, Bilawal reiterated his call to regain the opposition’s “lost trust” in the system and for the government to undertake confidence-building measures (CBMs) to convince it that institutions such as the Parliament, the Election Commission of Pakistan, the courts, and the accountability system could be trusted again.
Responding to a query, Bilawal reiterated that the disputed reserved seats of the 2024 elections were a “low-hanging fruit” through which the opposition’s trust could be restored and that could serve as a CBM.
The PTI was deprived of its reserved seats in the 2024 elections following the revocation of its electoral symbol.
“Political parties will have to negotiate among themselves, or approach the court, or carry out reforms, so this gets done,” Bilawal suggested, adding that he was “not happy that another party” does not have its electoral symbol today.
“Personally, I believe they (PTI) should have received the reserved seats,” said Bilawal, whose PPP was one of the parties to challenge the Supreme Court ruling that declared PTI eligible for those seats.
However, in a deviation from that, Bilawal today said, “When PTI itself is saying that I am PTI, then we should listen to what they say and acknowledge it. However, that did not happen.”
Referring to the SC Constitutional Bench’s 2025 order that overturned the July 2024 ruling and consequently handed the seats to the coalition parties, Bilawal said, “That order, in my opinion, was also slightly extra.”
He said that even if his stance was that PTI should not get the reserved seats based on the argument that their leaders did not contest as PTI candidates, “how can those seats be given to me or the PML-N or JUI?”
Bilawal added: “According to that very argument, the argument on which you say that PTI cannot get [reserved seats] because they are not PTI, they are SIC, then I am not PTI either, JUI is not PTI, PML-N is not PTI.”
The PPP chairman stressed he was only raising this point because he believed if the opposition’s trust is to be restored, the government will have to think about such “goodwill gestures”.
‘All decisions have to be made in Parliament alone’
At the same time, the PPP chairman highlighted a similar “lack of trust” in the opposition on the government’s part and urged the opposition to view its role as a responsibility rather than a choice.
“They should sit in our finance committee in the Parliament. When the government presents the budget, [they should give their] input,” he said, adding that the opposition’s role was essential to tackling unemployment, inflation and poverty.
“But they haven’t been doing it for quite some time. They come for the judiciary, but there is also a committee for the judiciary, they do not sit in that either. They are not present in all committees of the Parliament,” Bilawal pointed out.
He called on the opposition to review if they benefitted from their boycott of such parliamentary initiatives, urging that it take part in the recently proposed electoral reforms committee.
“Until there are free and fair elections, until our country, our nation, all provinces, and all parties have confidence in this electoral system, there will be no trust, and the governments formed or referendums held as a result of that electoral system will lack legitimacy and strength,” Bilawal asserted.
The PPP chairman reiterated his stance that any “reset or reboot of our system” should be democratic and political, with all political stakeholders, including the opposition, made part of the conversation.
“If we want to make the Parliament functional, then all decisions have to be made in the Parliament alone,” he said, adding that decisions taken “outside the Parliament” would leave the system incapable of overcoming challenges.
Regarding a proposed “reset” in the country, Bilawal said even though he had not started the conversation, the discussion was welcomed.
“This country’s system has collapsed. I believe we should take this conversation forward: if the system has collapsed, why is that? How did it happen? Did it happen on its own? Because of political workers? Because of political parties?” he questioned.
“If you do not want to determine the responsibility for it, then don’t. But if you’re sick and I need to get you treated, then first I have to diagnose you to figure out what the illness is.”
He stressed that unless the stakeholders together identified why the system collapsed, there would be difficulties reviving it.
Recalling his Nov 17 remarks, Bilawal said the “system, meaning the 1973 Constitution, did not collapse on its own, it was made to collapse again and again”.
The PPP chairman called for a debate to identify the “diagnosis” with consensus, adding that a “reset should not be decided by a few former and current media anchors, media owners, and a few people belonging to business sectors, as they would not draw attention to people’s real issues”.
At one point, Bilawal remarked that nationalist parties in Sindh label PPP as “wafaaqparast” (pro-Centre) and those in Islamabad “affectionately call us “qoumparast” (nationalist), but his party was “Pakistan-parast and awaamparast”.
‘You can’t have a hard state with thin skin’
The Bhutto scion further said, “This country should have enough courage that we can listen to the truth and tolerate each other’s differences of opinion.”
He termed the reactions to his Nov 17 press conference as “inappropriate”, contending that there were patriotic Pakistanis on both sides of an argument even if they had differing opinions.
Bilawal continued: “If anyone thinks that we can be silenced through such cheap tactics, that is not possible.
“It is now said these days that ‘Pakistan is a hard state’. For Pakistan to be that, the hard state should have thick skin. You can’t have a hard state with thin skin.”
Responding to a question, Bilawal said he not only condemned the recent actions against the Jamiat Ulema-i-Islam-Fazl chief during his stay in Lahore, but also termed them “incomprehensible”.
“If you will take such revengeful actions even against Maulana Fazlur Rehman, then do you expect them to clap for you or praise you?” he remarked.
The PPP chairman argued that as an opposition member, it was Fazl’s “patriotic duty” to criticise the government. “We should have a thick skin and absorb that criticism,” he said, adding that any suggestion in it that could lead to better decisions should be incorporated.
‘Non-existent press freedom’
During his address, the PPP chairman also highlighted the importance of a free press, adding that he wanted to restore the “power” previously held by press clubs across the country.
“With your voice, your columns, you used to make and topple governments,” the Bhutto scion remarked.
“I don’t want to topple any government, nor do I want to form another one. But I believe your role is to hold up a mirror to those sitting in power,” he added.
Bilawal said he was not denying “mistakes” made by him over time, insisting that his party’s intention was not to weaken the press.
He referred to the Prevention of Electronic Crimes Act (Peca), which was amended in January 2025 despite countrywide protests by journalists’ bodies.
He remarked that regardless of the PPP’s intention, the country had now arrived at a point where press freedom was non-existent.
The PPP chairman noted that if the media was given space only to regurgitate what those in power talked about among themselves, then issues related to economic crises and difficulties faced by the people at the grassroots level would not come to the forefront.
‘27th Amendment a success but judicial system lacks trust’
Bilawal also elaborated on his earlier remarks about the 26th and 27th Constitutional Amendments, insisting that an impression was being given as if he was backing away from them or criticising them.
In his Nov 17 remark, the PPP chairman, who had strongly advocated for the amendments at the time of their passage, had said the 27th Amendment was “passed neither through political consensus nor bilateral agreement; it was passed with brute majority”.
He had also questioned the current status of the judiciary, particularly in the wake of the 27th Amendment, noting that resignations of senior judges followed the legislation.
The next day, the PPP chairman clarified that he was “not disowning” the 27th Amendment, but rather noting that it did not enjoy the same “unanimous consensus” as the 18th Amendment and the 1973 Constitution.
“It’s not black and white,” Bilawal said today, stressing that the 1973 Constitution and the 18th Amendment were passed through mutual consensus.
He defended that the 26th Amendment was passed with as much consensus as could be developed and declared the passage of the 27th Amendment with a two-thirds majority a “success”.
“But, at the same time, I believe we cannot ignore the fact today that our opposition does not have confidence in this judicial system,” Bilawal stated, adding that judges and lawyers he interacts with are also “not comfortable” with the current status of the judiciary.
“Does this mean I am saying we should tear it up? No. I am merely asking what steps we can take, how can dialogue be held with all stakeholders, so that we can restore that lost trust?”
The PPP chairman clarified that while an attempt was made to make his “brute majority” remarks on the 27th Amendment controversial, as if he had meant force or used the word “brutal”, he had simply referred to the two-thirds majority that the legislation had.
‘Accountability system used solely for political opponents’
During his address, the PPP chairman also doubled down on his criticism of the country’s accountability system, asserting that its structure was “based on political victimisation”
“Unfortunately, no matter how big the claims the governments came in with, the more emphasis they placed on eradicating corruption, the more corruption there has been,” he remarked.
Bilawal contended that despite strong rhetoric by governments on ending corruption, they failed to do so as the accountability system was “used solely against political opponents”.
The PPP chairman regretted that ongoing discussions in the Parliament, the federal cabinet, seminars, or even television talk shows did not pay attention to the fundamental issues faced by the public.
“People are distressed about managing two square meals. They’re worried whether they should educate their children or arrange medicines for their elders,” he said.
Bilawal noted there was “restlessness and distress” among Pakistan’s youth, who faced unemployment and, even if employed, their salaries were spent on just commuting to and from work.
“If we truly want this system to serve the public, want our country to be capable of facing every problem, whether they are economic hardships or foreign difficulties, then we will have to take steps as soon as possible so we can establish unity, political stability, and restore trust in all institutions,” he stated.
